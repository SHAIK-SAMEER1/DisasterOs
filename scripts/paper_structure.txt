[005] Abstract
[006] DisasterOS is a modular disaster-management platform designed to reduce the coordination gap between hazard detection and field response. The system combines citizen incident reporting, live weather and seismic telemetry, geospatial visualization, emergency-resource coordination, and a deterministic risk engine in a shared operational workflow. The current implementation uses an ingestion gateway for Open-Meteo rainfall data and the USGS real-time earthquake feed, a zone-level risk engine based on rainfall, seismic magnitude, vulnerability, and shelter headroom, and a client-side data layer for operational records. The application exposes 25 functional modules covering command operations, incidents, emergency help, dispatch, SOP workflows, evacuation, shelters, hospitals, volunteers, inventory, relief distribution, infrastructure, analytics, AI-assisted reporting, access control, audit, and system health. Review 1 focuses on the implemented core software, database structures, algorithms, and system architecture required for the next development phase. The design deliberately favors transparent rules and explicit thresholds so that operators can understand why a zone is classified as advisory, watch, warning, or emergency. Remaining work includes production-grade persistence, deeper automation, and broader field validation.

[012] 1. Introduction
[013] Disaster response often fails not because useful information is unavailable, but because information is fragmented across agencies, dashboards, field teams, and resource registers. A rainfall warning may reach an operator without immediately translating into a verified incident, a prioritized rescue request, an available shelter, or a hospital bed allocation. DisasterOS addresses this coordination problem by treating the response workflow as a connected software system rather than a collection of isolated monitoring tools.
[014] The proposed platform combines public hazard feeds, citizen reports, operational resource data, and role-specific interfaces around a common data-access layer. Its risk calculation is intentionally deterministic. Operators can inspect the individual inputs, adjust thresholds, and reproduce the score without training or retraining a machine-learning model. The current implementation also provides a GIS view, operational dashboards, emergency-request handling, response-team coordination, resource tracking, reporting, audit logging, and role-based access.
[015] The study is guided by three practical questions: (1) how can heterogeneous hazard and operational data be brought into one consistent operational picture; (2) how can a transparent zone-risk score translate incoming readings into actionable priorities; and (3) how can the resulting priorities be carried through incident, resource, and response workflows without creating separate data silos?
[016] The main contribution at Review 1 is a concrete implementation contract: the system structure, module boundaries, data model, risk and prioritization algorithms, and the corresponding architecture, DFD, use-case, component, and sequence views. The report also records the present software status so that later reviews can measure implementation progress against a stable baseline.
[019] 2. Literature Survey and Related Work
[020] Several established systems address important parts of disaster information management. Sahana and its later Sahana Eden platform provide a broad operational scope covering organizations, human resources, warehousing, hospitals, shelters, and geospatial information [1]. Their main relevance to DisasterOS is the idea that disaster operations benefit from a shared information model rather than isolated departmental tools.
[021] Ushahidi established a widely used pattern for rapid, geolocated citizen reporting and crisis mapping [2]. Its strength is fast aggregation of crowd-sourced information, while structured resource coordination is outside its original emphasis. DisasterOS carries the citizen-reporting idea further by connecting incident intake to operational data such as teams, shelters, hospitals, and relief resources.
[022] FEMA HAZUS provides a structured GIS-based methodology for multi-hazard loss estimation and planning [3,4]. Compared with that planning-oriented approach, DisasterOS focuses on a lightweight operational pipeline that can consume current readings and update a per-zone risk classification during an active event.
[023] The resulting design combines the coordination breadth associated with Sahana Eden, the low-friction reporting model associated with Ushahidi, and the explicit quantitative reasoning associated with HAZUS. Its specific focus is a single rule-based pipeline connecting live feeds, operational records, role dashboards, and response prioritization using openly accessible data sources.
[025] 3. System Architecture & Methodology
[026] The DisasterOS architecture is organized as a layered platform. At the outer boundary are operational actors and external data services. The presentation layer provides the citizen portal, command-center views, GIS visualization, and assistant interface. The application layer contains the main orchestrator and services for incidents, emergency requests, resources, and notifications. The core intelligence layer ingests telemetry, computes zone risk, applies decision and SOP logic, and prioritizes response work. The data layer provides CRUD operations and stores shared operational state. This separation keeps interface concerns distinct from risk and resource algorithms.
[027] The current implementation uses App Orchestrator (app.js), IngestionGateway (gateway.js), GISMapController (map.js), RiskEngine (risk-engine.js), and DataStore (store.js) as its main software units. The browser application exposes 25 functional modules. The data layer maintains operational collections for zones, incidents, emergency requests, shelters, hospitals, volunteers, inventory, infrastructure, relief deliveries, teams, alerts, and audit logs. Browser local storage is the current persistence mechanism; PostgreSQL/Supabase is the planned target for multi-device operation.

[088] The current application exposes 25 functional modules. The module list below is kept complete so that the report matches the implemented application navigation and review scope.
[090] 4.1 Zone-Risk Calculation
[091] For each monitored zone, the RiskEngine normalizes rainfall and seismic inputs, combines them with static vulnerability and shelter headroom, and then clamps the result to [0,1]. The current weights are 0.35 for rainfall, 0.30 for seismic activity, 0.25 for vulnerability, and 0.20 for shelter headroom.
[092] R_zone = 0.35 × Rain_norm + 0.30 × Seismic_norm + 0.25 × Vulnerability − 0.20 × ShelterHeadroom
[093] (1)
[094] Rainfall is normalized against a 50 mm / 3-hour reference and seismic activity against magnitude 7.0. ShelterHeadroom is 1 − occupied/total capacity when capacity data are available. The implemented thresholds classify R_zone ≥ 0.85 as emergency, 0.65–<0.85 as warning, 0.45–<0.65 as watch, 0.25–<0.45 as advisory, and values below 0.25 as baseline.
[095] 4.2 Emergency-Request Priority
[096] Open emergency requests are scored using need-type urgency, the current risk of the request's zone, and a logarithmic load penalty based on the number of people involved.
[097] Priority = 0.50 × Urgency + 0.35 × ZoneRisk + 0.15 × LoadPenalty
[098] (2)
[099] The urgency mapping gives the highest weight to rescue, followed by medical, evacuation, water, food, and shelter requests. The logarithmic load term prevents large population counts from dominating the score by themselves. Requests are sorted in descending order of computed priority.
[100] 4.3 Telemetry and GIS Processing
[101] The ingestion gateway caches weather readings for five minutes and seismic readings for one minute. Open-Meteo precipitation and current weather values are normalized into internal readings, while the USGS GeoJSON feed is filtered by distance using the Haversine formula. The GIS controller renders risk zones, incidents, shelters, hospitals, and seismic events through Leaflet layers.
[103] 5. Implementation and Experimental Setup
[104] The current prototype is implemented as a browser-based application using HTML, CSS, and JavaScript. app.js manages the application shell and navigation; gateway.js handles live weather and seismic acquisition; map.js handles geospatial visualization; risk-engine.js contains the risk and priority algorithms; and store.js manages operational data and audit records.
[105] The implemented data path begins at the user interface or citizen report form, passes through application and telemetry logic, evaluates risk and priorities, updates the data layer, and returns refreshed state to the dashboards. The store contains zones, incidents, emergency requests, shelters, hospitals, volunteers, inventory, infrastructure, relief deliveries, teams, alerts, and audit logs. Browser local storage is the present persistence mechanism.
[106] The experimental setup for the next review will use fixture or recorded Open-Meteo and USGS payloads so that ingestion and risk calculations can be verified independently of network availability. Test scenarios will vary rainfall, seismic magnitude, vulnerability, shelter occupancy, request type, and people count. The objective is to establish correctness first and then measure end-to-end response behavior.

[118] 7. Preliminary Results and Discussion
[119] At Review 1, the principal result is functional coverage rather than a numerical performance claim. The software demonstrates a consistent separation between interface, ingestion, GIS, risk, and storage responsibilities. A region change updates map context and monitored-zone coordinates, followed by a telemetry synchronization pass that evaluates weather, seismic activity, shelter capacity, and zone vulnerability.
[120] The deterministic algorithms also provide a straightforward verification path because identical fixture inputs lead to reproducible risk classifications and priority orderings. Recorded weather and earthquake payloads can therefore be replayed without depending on a live network. The broad module surface additionally supports scenario walkthroughs across command operations, emergency response, resources, intelligence, citizen services, and governance.
[121] No fabricated performance result is reported. Response-time reduction, throughput, and concurrency measurements should be obtained only after the test deployment exists. The next review can therefore extend this work with controlled latency measurements and end-to-end workflow tests while retaining the same algorithms and data contracts.
[123] 8. Conclusion and Future Scope
[124] DisasterOS presents a unified software architecture for disaster early warning, incident coordination, resource management, and operational decision support. Its central design choice is a transparent rule-based risk engine that combines live weather and seismic signals with zone vulnerability and shelter headroom. The resulting risk state can support emergency-request prioritization, dispatch-oriented workflows, and role-specific operational views.
[125] The Review 1 implementation includes the application shell, 25 functional modules, live feed integration paths, GIS visualization, risk and priority algorithms, operational browser storage, role management, and audit support. Future work includes production relational persistence, deeper SOP automation, richer medical and infrastructure telemetry, more resilient offline field operation, and evaluation of dispatch assignment strategies beyond the current deterministic heuristic.
